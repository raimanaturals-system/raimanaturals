Satkora

Citrus macroptera Montr. — Sylhet’s beef-curry citrus

A large, thick-rinded semi-wild citrus of the Northeast India–Sylhet hill country, grown less for its fruit than for the intensely aromatic rind that defines a signature Sylheti dish. Unlike gondhoraj, its botanical identity and chemistry are already published — what Raima still has to prove is where, and how, we can responsibly source it.

01At a glance

Satkora — also written shatkora, hatkora or hatkhora (Sylheti/Bengali শাতকরা/হাতকরা), and known to English-language botany as the Melanesian papeda or wild orange — is a large, thorny, semi-wild citrus from the old papeda lineage, a group that sits near the root of the entire Citrus family tree. Where the gondhoraj’s very identity is contested, satkora’s is not: essentially every peer-reviewed study out of Northeast India treats it as a single, settled species, Citrus macroptera Montr., named for the oversized “winged” petiole beneath each leaf.

What distinguishes it commercially is scale and use. The fruit runs 6–12.5 cm across — large for a citrus — with a thick, fleshy, intensely aromatic rind up to 1.75 cm deep that is the entire point of growing it. The pulp is dry and bitter by reputation, though measured juice content actually swings from 13% to 82% across wild seedling trees; what holds true across all of them is that nobody grows satkora for its juice. They grow it for the rind.

That rind already has a proven market. It is the signature ingredient in beef shatkora, a slow-cooked Sylheti dish carried across the world by one of the largest, most concentrated diaspora communities in food retail — British Bangladeshis in the UK. A documented essential-oil trade barely exists yet. That gap, not the fruit’s identity, is where Raima’s work begins.

02Olfactive profile

Bright citrus over a dry, spicy wood.

The two dedicated commercial oils on the market today describe satkora peel oil the same way: spicy, resinous and creamy, a dry citrus that echoes the sharpness of kaffir lime but sits softer and deeper underneath it. It reads as citrus first, then turns toward a warm, faintly bitter woody-spice register that is genuinely uncommon in Western perfumery — and the working niche perfumers who do use it say plainly that supply, not interest, is what's holding it back.

Worth saying clearly: satkora is not simply kaffir lime by another name. Kaffir lime leaf oil is built almost entirely around one molecule, citronellal (roughly 78%). Satkora's leaf oil runs a completely different register — sabinene, β-pinene and linalool — while its peel oil is dominated instead by limonene. Two citrus fruits, two distinct chemical fingerprints.

03The GC-MS lens

A citrus the literature has already characterised — unusually, for us.

Satkora is the rare Raima botanical where we start from a real, published chemotype rather than a blank page. Multiple independent GC-MS studies — from Northeast India, Mizoram, Bangladesh and beyond — agree the peel oil is limonene-dominant. They disagree, sometimes sharply, on how dominant: reported limonene content swings from roughly 23% to 87% of the oil depending on the study and the individual tree. That spread is real, not measurement error — satkora is still unselected, seed-grown wild germplasm, so no two trees are chemically identical yet.

Published yields span roughly 0.11–1.9% w/w — a spread driven as much by extraction method as by biology [5]. One 2025 study lifted yield 3–6× over a conventional baseline using sonication-assisted hydrodistillation alone, before any change to the fruit — process engineering is a real, testable lever here, not noise. No cold-press data exists yet for this species, despite cold-press being the industry norm for citrus peel: a genuine white space.

Raima's note on these numbers

These figures come from published studies of Citrus macroptera grown in Mizoram, Bangladesh, New Caledonia and Portugal — not yet from our own material, and not from Tripura-grown fruit at all. Treat every number above as a bracket, not a specification.

Before any of this reaches a buyer, we GC-MS our own feedstock and test both conventional and intensified extraction ourselves — the same discipline we apply to every botanical in this library.

04Botany & identity

Settled as a species — unsettled as a rank.

Kew's Plants of the World Online and GBIF's taxonomic backbone currently list Citrus macroptera Montr. as a synonym of Citrus hystrix — true kaffir lime — at the level of formal nomenclature. Almost none of the applied agricultural or phytochemical literature follows that lumping: ICAR, NBPGR and UC Riverside's citrus collection all treat it as its own species, because commercially and agriculturally it is a completely different thing from kaffir lime — a large, thick-rind curry fruit, not a small, bumpy leaf crop. We use Citrus macroptera Montr. because that is what the working science uses and it correctly identifies the plant, but a technical buyer working from Kew's accepted taxonomy could reasonably raise the point — we'd rather flag that ourselves than be caught out by it.

Three varieties are on record — annamensis, kerrii and combara — and it is specifically the annamensis oil that the literature ties to perfumery use, which matters for knowing exactly what we would be propagating. Satkora should not be confused with true kaffir lime (Citrus hystrix, grown for leaf), with Citrus micrantha (a different ancestral papeda), or with Citrus macrophylla / alemow — an unrelated rootstock species that shares only a similar-sounding name.

05Culinary & cultural significance

An identity ingredient before it is anything else.

Long before satkora is an essential oil, it is a name that signals where a family is from. Beef shatkora — beef slow-cooked with warm spice and chunks of the citrus rind, which soften and release a sharp, faintly bitter perfume into the dish — is the signature preparation, alongside mutton, fish and dal versions across Sylheti, Bangladeshi and Northeast Indian kitchens. In the UK, where the British-Bangladeshi community runs the large majority of the country's Indian restaurant trade, a “beef shatkora” on the menu is a specific, legible marker of Sylheti origin — not a generic curry-house flourish.

That translates directly into retail: fresh, frozen, sun-dried and pickled satkora already sell across at least nine distinct UK product lines, from sliced fresh fruit to dried “shutki” at a real premium per kilo. In other words, this is already a proven ingredient market — just not yet an essential-oil one. For an aroma house, that ordering matters: the rind's food value is established and documented; the oil is the newer, smaller, still-forming opportunity sitting alongside it.

06Terroir, conservation & sourcing

Grows wild in Tripura's hills — sourcing still to be proven.

Citrus macroptera is documented growing semi-wild in Tripura, centred on the Jampui Hills — the same range known for Tripura's oranges — alongside populations across Assam's Barak Valley, Mizoram and Sylhet in Bangladesh, the crop's cultural heartland. Wild populations are regionally reported as declining, driven by shifting cultivation and forest clearing rather than any formal endangerment listing — there is no IUCN Red List assessment and no CITES listing for the species. That points to the same principle we apply across this library: build on cultivated stock, not wild collection.

Founder's note on sourcing

We want to be precise here rather than let a good provenance story get ahead of the facts. Satkora growing wild in Tripura's hills is well documented — but an organised commercial production base in Tripura is not, today. The strongest existing cultivation and trade centres for this crop sit in Mizoram, Assam's Barak Valley and Sylhet.

Our founding-stage work is to establish whether we can secure cultivated satkora feedstock in or near Tripura at a workable price — or whether this becomes a sourced-and-processed line drawing on the wider Northeast/Sylhet growing region. Until that question is answered, we are not presenting satkora as a local-estate crop the way we can with gondhoraj.

07Market reality

A proven food ingredient; a barely-formed oil market.

The diaspora fruit and peel trade is real, sizeable and already commercialised, as the previous section shows. The essential-oil side of the ledger looks completely different: only two dedicated sellers of a satkora-type oil surface anywhere — one working from Portugal-grown trees, one selling India-sourced oil at hobbyist dropper prices — alongside a working niche perfumer who states plainly that satkora “remains rare in Western perfumery due to limited commercial availability.” That is a buyer telling us, unprompted, that supply — not demand — is the binding constraint in the niche-natural segment.

Our reading: the fastest, lowest-risk opportunity is the documented rind and extract lane that already has proven buyers, not a jump straight to commodity essential oil. A small, high-margin niche oil for perfumers who are already telling the market they can't get supply is a real, separate opportunity worth pursuing in parallel — just not at commodity-oil economics, and not before we've measured our own yield and secured our own feedstock.
